Skepticism about Consciousness in Animals without Language

23 Oct 2026
Philosophy
Essay
Philosophy of Mind

In this essay, “language” is restricted to the “Faculty of Language in the Narrow Sense,” that is, language with a recursive structure. The dances of honeybees, the olfaction of dogs, and the acoustic signals of whales share certain features of language in that they serve as means of communication, but they are not language in the “narrow” sense. Recursive language appears to be found only in humans.

Lately I have been learning some perplexing facts about consciousness. They all point towards the thesis that the capacity to use language is required for a certain level of consciousness. Here are a few examples:

  • Helen Keller, who lost her sight and hearing at nineteen months and acquired language only around the age of seven, recalled that before she acquired language she did not even know that she existed. Similar recollections are common among adults who acquired language late.
  • Deaf children raised without access to sign language show delayed development in understanding mental states. For example, they fail the Sally–Anne test. Once they acquire vocabulary for mental states, however, they pass. [1]
  • Childhood amnesia is the phenomenon whereby most adults cannot recall events from before the age of three or four. The end of childhood amnesia coincides with the period of language acquisition.

Of course, these reports admit different interpretations. On a moderate interpretation, pre-linguistic subjects are conscious but simply fail to remember their conscious experiences. But a more radical interpretation is also available: whatever lacks language also lacks consciousness. This would suggest that most animals other than humans are not conscious. And there are evidences supporting this conclusion:

  • There are many cases of apes being taught sign language, and they sometimes answer the experimenters’ questions correctly. But there are almost no reports of an ape asking the experimenter a question first.
  • Frogs whose optic nerves have been rewired strike at moving prey in the mirror-reversed direction, yet still avoid stationary obstacles accurately. [2]

The former case suggests that apes do not understand that “the other” might know what “I” do not, and perhaps that they lack the very ability to distinguish self from other. The latter case suggests that the frog’s prey-catching and obstacle-avoidance do not rest on a shared conscious visual experience.

But isn’t the claim that animals lack consciousness absurd? Just look at our beloved dogs and cats; don’t they perform behaviors so complex and sophisticated that we cannot think of them as unconscious?

This is an intuitive objection, but we should not forget the many cases showing that complex behavior is not necessarily accompanied by conscious experience:

  • Sleepwalkers perform complex actions such as cooking, talking, and driving while in deep sleep, and remember nothing after waking up. (A strking case is a sleepwalker who drove more than 20 km and killed his mother-in-law in his sleep.)
  • Some patients with damage to the visual cortex report that they cannot see objects in a certain region of their visual field, yet accurately point to or avoid them. (This is known as “blindsight”.)
  • The case of Patient DF: when presented with an angled slot, she could neither verbalize its orientation nor imitate it with her hand, yet she could accurately slip a card through it.

What should we conclude from these puzzling facts? Here is my conclusion.

Some animals have no form of consciousness at all. Some animals may have a limited form of consciousness, such as sentience. But no animal other than humans has self-consciousness, or apperception.

Suppose you prick an animal with a needle. It will show some kind of pain response. But this may be a purely behavioral stimulus-response phenomenon, no different from the motion of a robot. On the other hand, some animals may really feel the sensation of pain. Even so, it seems that no animal understands this situation as “I am in pain.” (The same seems to hold for newborns.) The mental world of animals is therefore a kind of communism. There, the notion of individual consciousness is nonexistent.

This thought unsettles me. I love animals. I have a dog, I try to practice a rudimentary vegetarianism, and I think animal rights should be expanded. Behind these attitudes lies the belief that animals, too, have conscious experiences comparable to our own. But once this belief is seriously called into question, where should my attitude toward animals find new grounds? Or must the attitude itself be revised?

Let me close with a concrete instance of this worry. If my tentative view that animals have sentience but not self-consciousness is correct, then it seems to follow that making animals suffer is immoral, but killing them (without pain) is not. For it seems that to have a right to life, that is, the right not to be killed, one must at least be able to conceive of oneself as a continuing subject of experiences. [3] This is why we take plants to have life but no right to life, and it is also a ground for the ethical permissibility of abortion. So if animals lack self-consciousness, then arguments based on physiological & psychological facts about animals may justify their right not to suffer, but cannot justify their right to life.

Note. Since it is possible both to inflict pain without killing (torture) and to kill without inflicting pain (euthanasia), killing and suffering are mutually independent notions. Hence whether animals feel pain and whether it is permissible to kill them are (at least logically) unrelated questions.

Therefore, if we wish to justify a right to life for animals, it seems we must look for its grounds beyond the physiological & psychological facts about them. The question, then, is whether we can justify animals’ right to life without thereby attaching moral responsibility to cultivating plants or to abortion, and whether this fine maneuver can succeed without falling into relativism, moral skepticism, or dogmatism. This is where ethics proper begins.
